Engineering Curiosity & Practice

How I approach engineering outside the syllabus: learning systems from the ground up, tracing mathematical physics to hardware execution, and staying curious about physical mechanisms.

REFLECTIVE PRACTICE 01

I Learn by Building

It is easy to import a high-level library, pass a mesh into a black-box solver, and plot colorful stress contours. But that does not teach you why a simulation diverges, where memory bandwidth is squandered, or how numerical stencils preserve physical invariants.

When I set out to build DynaMesh-Thermal, I deliberately decided not to use commercial CFD wrappers. I wanted to understand C++ memory allocation, write CUDA kernels, manage thread hierarchies, and experience the pain of sparse matrix index arrays (CSR). Implementing the Finite Volume Method from scratch on the GPU forced me to grapple directly with flux balances, pressure Poisson stencils, and GPU cache lines. Building tools from first principles turns abstract equations into deep, intuitive engineering instincts.

REFLECTIVE PRACTICE 02

I Like Understanding What's Underneath

EQUATIONS → DISCRETIZATION → IMPLEMENTATION → PHYSICAL BEHAVIOUR

My favorite moment in any project is connecting an analytical equation to observable physical behaviour through code.

In MicroWear-PINN, the biharmonic Airy stress equation ($\nabla^4 \Phi = 0$) initially looks like pure abstract mathematics. But when you enforce that operator across a spatial domain and watch the internal shear stress contours align naturally with classical Hertzian contact mechanics—without feeding in internal stress sensors—the mathematical consistency of continuum mechanics becomes astonishingly tangible.

REFLECTIVE PRACTICE 03

Mechanical Engineering Keeps Me Curious

Mechanical engineering is fundamentally honest. A mechanism either transfers torque without binding, or it jams. A thermal exchanger either dissipates the heat flux, or the component melts. A boundary layer either remains attached, or flow separates.

I am fascinated by how machines, thermal systems, mechanisms, and manufacturing processes operate in the physical world. Whether analyzing the kinematic linkages of a human knee in gait dynamics, studying the shear stresses inside a sliding contact joint, or evaluating fluid channels in heat sinks, mechanical engineering provides an endlessly rich catalog of real-world problems demanding creative, rigorous computational solutions.
